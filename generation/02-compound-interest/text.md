## How compound interest works

With compound interest you earn interest on your interest. Each year's interest is added to the balance, and next year's interest is calculated on that larger balance. With simple interest, by contrast, you only ever earn on the original amount.

The formula for annual compounding is:

```
A = P × (1 + r)^t
```

where `P` is the starting amount, `r` the yearly rate and `t` the number of years.

### $5,000 at 6% for 20 years

```
A = 5,000 × 1.06^20 = 5,000 × 3.2071 = $16,035.68
```

| Year | Balance | Interest earned that year |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | $5,000.00 | — |
| 1 | $5,300.00 | $300.00 |
| 5 | $6,691.13 | $378.74 |
| 10 | $8,954.24 | $506.84 |
| 15 | $11,982.79 | $678.27 |
| 20 | $16,035.68 | $907.68 |

Your $5,000 becomes **$16,035.68**: you put in $5,000 and interest adds **$11,035.68**.

### Why it matters

- **The yearly interest keeps growing.** It is $300 in year 1 and $907.68 in year 20, even though the rate never changes.
- **Simple interest would give much less.** At 6% simple interest you would earn a flat $300 a year and finish with $11,000. Compounding adds an extra **$5,035.68**.
- **Rule of 72.** Divide 72 by the rate to estimate the doubling time: 72 ÷ 6 = 12 years. The balance does pass $10,000 during year 12.
- **Time does most of the work.** More than half of the total interest ($5,974.70 of $11,035.68) arrives in the last eight years, which is why starting early beats contributing more later.
